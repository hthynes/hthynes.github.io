Quantitative sociology · education policy · inequality

I study how institutions classify people, allocate resources, and produce inequality.

I’m a sociology researcher at the University of Central Florida. I study education, disability, race, and public policy, with a particular interest in the decisions institutions make before an outcome ever appears in a dataset. I use administrative and population data to ask where disparities emerge and what the available evidence can actually tell us.

Research agenda

I keep coming back to the same question: where does the disparity begin?

01

Classification systems

I study who gets identified, how people are sorted into categories, and what changes when those categories are defined differently.

02

Institutional allocation

I look at the rules and decisions between identifying a need and actually receiving support, including how institutions decide where resources go.

03

Unequal outcomes

I want to know where racial, socioeconomic, and disability-related differences first appear, how they change across a system, and how much of that pattern the data can explain.

Current work

When Speech and Language Diverge

My honors thesis uses eight years of Florida Department of Education administrative data across all 67 county school districts to examine racial and ethnic patterns in Speech Impairment and Language Impairment program membership separately.

I built the analytic panel from 16 state workbooks and wrote the pipeline to reproduce and check it. The analysis also tests what happens when I change the denominator, district threshold, and treatment of suppressed cells. The outcome is program membership, so I do not treat these data as a measure of who actually has a speech or language disorder.

What connects the work

A result can change before a model ever runs.

A category can combine things that work differently. A denominator can change the comparison. A reporting rule can erase a distinction. I want to understand those choices well enough to know what a result means, and to leave enough of an audit trail that someone else can check my work.

Methods

The question comes before the method

My current empirical work is primarily quantitative, but the research program I am building is multi-method. I want to combine quantitative and longitudinal evidence with historical archives and community-engaged research to study how racism, ableism, white supremacy, and socioeconomic inequality become embedded in educational institutions and shape Black disabled young people’s lives.

Evidence strategy

Different evidence sees different parts of the story

01Administrative + survey data

Patterns, disparities, timing, trajectories, and policy change at scale.

The question decides the evidence. No single data source gets to stand in for the whole life of a disabled young person.

In Who Gets Help First?, I pool the 2023 and 2024 survey files, account for the survey design, work with the multiply imputed household-poverty measures, fit regression models, and estimate adjusted probabilities.

How I think about evidence

Institutional records are records of institutions

A disability category in an administrative dataset tells me how an institution recorded a student under particular rules and practices. I do not assume that the category is a complete or neutral measure of disability itself. This distinction shapes how I define variables, interpret disparities, write limitations, and decide what other evidence a question requires.

Research workflow

From source files to results I can rebuild

I use Git to track changes and write projects so I can rerun the cleaning, checks, analysis, tables, and figures from code. I keep records of the original files and the changes I make to them, and I check for duplicate IDs, impossible values, failed merges, and other problems that could change the results.

Building toward

A multi-method research practice

I want deeper training in longitudinal and multilevel models, event-history and sequence analysis, record linkage, and causal and quasi-experimental designs so I can study how institutional experiences accumulate across the life course. I also want formal training in archival and historical research so I can trace how educational institutions, disability policy, and ideas about normalcy were built and changed over time. Community-engaged approaches matter to me because institutional records cannot tell me how young people understand, navigate, or resist the environments around them.

Methods

How I work

Most of my research right now is quantitative. I work mainly in R with administrative and survey data, and I’ve learned to spend a lot of time on the unglamorous part: definitions, denominators, missing values, suppressed cells, merges, and all the other things that can quietly change a result.

Before the model

I want to know where the variable came from.

With administrative data, that usually means reading the documentation before writing the analysis.

  • What exactly is being counted?
  • Who is missing?
  • Did the definition change?
  • What was suppressed or combined?
  • What can I actually say from this?

Complex surveys

National Survey of Children’s Health

In Who Gets Help First?, I pool the 2023 and 2024 survey files, account for the survey design, work with the multiply imputed household-poverty measures, fit regression models, and estimate adjusted probabilities.

Florida special-education data

For my honors thesis, I combine 16 FLDOE workbooks, check district-year-race keys and totals, calculate program-membership rates and risk ratios, and rerun the analysis under alternative denominators, district cutoffs, and assumptions about suppressed cells.

For The Staying Power of Special Education, I use state-by-year data and a staggered difference-in-differences design to estimate changes in IDEA identification after school Medicaid expansions.

Reproducibility

I want to be able to rerun my own work.

I keep the original files, do the cleaning and analysis in code, and use Git to track changes. Before I trust a result, I check the boring things: duplicate IDs, bad merges, impossible values, missing data, and whether a reasonable change in definition changes the answer.

What I want to learn next

Methods for questions I cannot answer yet

There are questions I care about that my current toolkit cannot answer well. I want stronger training in longitudinal and multilevel models, event-history and sequence analysis, record linkage, and causal and quasi-experimental designs. I also want to learn archival, historical, and community-engaged research properly rather than treating administrative data as the answer to everything.

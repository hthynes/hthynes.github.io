From speech-language pathology to sociology

I went into college thinking I would become an SLP. Somewhere along the way, I became more interested in who gets labeled, who gets services, and who made the rules in the first place.
Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

I was born in Jamaica and grew up in Jamaica and the United States. I’m currently completing a B.S. in Sociology at UCF, with minors in Cultural Anthropology and Political Science.

I started college wanting to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I wanted to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities.

The more I learned about special education, the less satisfied I was with taking its categories for granted. I kept wondering who decided what counted as a disorder, why some students were identified and others were not, and how race and class shaped the answer. Eventually it became obvious that I was asking sociology questions.

Speech and language followed me anyway. My honors thesis uses Florida school records to compare Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, which is funny given how determined I once was to leave the field behind.

Sociology gave me room to ask the bigger questions I had been circling: how schools and professions decide what is normal, how those decisions became authoritative, and why race and disability keep showing up together in the distribution of support and punishment. I’ve found Black feminist scholarship, Critical Race Theory, and disability studies especially useful for thinking through those questions.

Most of my training so far is quantitative. I like working with large datasets, but I’m increasingly interested in the history behind the variables I use and in the parts of disabled young people’s lives that never make it into a spreadsheet. That is where I want my training to go next.

How I work

I’m suspicious of easy answers.

If a result depends on one coding choice, I want to know. If the data cannot answer the question, I would rather say that than make them do more than they can. I document my decisions, keep my quantitative work reproducible, and test alternatives when they matter.

From speech-language pathology to sociology

My training in speech-language pathology raised questions about how schools define disability and need, how those decisions vary across students and institutions, and how race and inequality shape the process.
Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

I was born in Jamaica and grew up in Jamaica and the United States. I’m currently completing a B.S. in Sociology at UCF, with minors in Cultural Anthropology and Political Science.

I started college wanting to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I wanted to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities.

As I learned more about special education, I became less interested in clinical practice and more interested in the institutions surrounding children. I became interested in how schools define disability and need, how racism, ableism, white supremacy, and class inequality shape those decisions, and how students experience the consequences. Those questions brought me to sociology.

Speech and language remain part of my current research. I’m looking at Florida because it reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, and I wanted to see what racial and ethnic patterns look like when the two classifications are not collapsed. I treat those categories as records of institutional decisions rather than as complete descriptions of disabled children themselves.

My broader research centers the educational experiences and life-course trajectories of Black disabled children and youth from low-resource communities. I rely heavily on the social model of disability and am drawn to Critical Disability Studies because they push me to examine disabling environments and relations of power rather than locating inequality only within individual bodies or diagnoses. I study how white supremacy, ableism, white supremacy, and socioeconomic inequality are reproduced through educational institutions and how young people experience and respond to those conditions.

I use quantitative methods and am developing training in historical, archival, and community-engaged research. Quantitative, administrative, and longitudinal data can help me identify patterns and follow trajectories across institutions and time. Historical and archival research can help me understand how those institutions, categories, and assumptions were built. Community-engaged research can help ensure that institutional records are not the only accounts of disabled young people’s lives. Across these approaches, I am interested in the policies and conditions that expand agency, opportunity, access, and self-determination.

How I work

Research approach

I try to begin with the social and institutional problem rather than the method or dataset. In quantitative work, I ask what a variable actually records before deciding what it can tell me about disability, race, or inequality. I keep my data work reproducible while building toward historical, archival, and community-engaged approaches that can answer questions institutional datasets cannot.

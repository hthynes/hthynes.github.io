From speech-language pathology to sociology

My training in speech-language pathology raised questions about how schools define disability and need, how those decisions vary across students and institutions, and how race and inequality shape the process.
Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

I was born in Jamaica and grew up in Jamaica and the United States. I’m currently completing a B.S. in Sociology at UCF, with minors in Cultural Anthropology and Political Science.

I started college wanting to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I wanted to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities.

The more I learned about special education, the more my attention shifted from clinical practice to the systems around it. I wanted to know who had the authority to define disability and need, how those judgments became school records and services, and why the answers were not the same for every child. That is what pulled me toward sociology.

I did not leave speech and language behind. My honors thesis brought me back to the field from a different angle: using Florida school records to ask what becomes visible when Speech Impairment and Language Impairment are kept separate.

That shift changed the questions I wanted to ask. I became interested in the schools, professions, policies, and histories around disability—not only in individual diagnosis or treatment. Work in Black feminist thought, Critical Race Theory, and disability studies has helped me make sense of questions I first encountered through practice.

Quantitative work is where I have the most training now. I want the next stage of my education to widen what I can see: longitudinal data for trajectories, archives for institutional history, and community-engaged work for experiences that never enter an administrative file.

Research approach

I like research that is careful about what the evidence can bear. In practice, that means documenting decisions, keeping quantitative work reproducible, testing alternatives when a result depends on a coding choice, and being explicit about what remains unanswered.

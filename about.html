I was born in Jamaica and grew up in Jamaica and the United States. I’m currently completing a B.S. in Sociology at UCF, with minors in Cultural Anthropology and Political Science.

I started college wanting to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I wanted to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities.

As I learned more about special education, I became less interested in clinical practice and more interested in the decisions schools make around disability and services. I kept wondering why some children made it into special education and others didn’t. Why Black and Latino children were more likely to end up in some disability categories than others. How much the school district, the resources available, or even the way a state defines a category could change what happened to a child. Those questions are what brought me to sociology.

Speech and language never really disappeared from my work, which is probably obvious from my honors thesis. I’m looking at Florida because it reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, and I wanted to see what the racial and ethnic patterns looked like when I didn’t collapse the two. That project has led me into other questions about developmental services, special-education data, and the way disability gets classified and reported.

I want my career to center Black and Latino children from low-resource communities. I’m still figuring out exactly what that research program will look like. I want to work with large administrative and longitudinal datasets that let me study how children move through schools and other public systems over time, including when they are identified for services, what support they receive, and how those experiences differ by race and socioeconomic status.

About

Taiel Lucile

they/them/él

I’m a sociology researcher at the University of Central Florida. I study the educational experiences and life-course outcomes of Black disabled children and youth from low-resource communities, and how race, disability, and socioeconomic inequality shape the institutions they encounter.

Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

I was born in Jamaica and grew up in Jamaica and the United States. I’m currently completing a B.S. in Sociology at UCF, with minors in Cultural Anthropology and Political Science.

I started college wanting to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I knew I wanted my work to matter for children whose needs and possibilities are too often constrained by the institutions around them.

As I learned more about special education, I became less interested in clinical practice and more interested in the institutions surrounding children. I kept wondering how schools decide what a child needs, how race and socioeconomic inequality shape those judgments, what happens after a child is identified as disabled, and how those experiences accumulate as young people move through school and into adulthood. Those questions are what brought me to sociology.

Speech and language never really disappeared from my work, which is probably obvious from my honors thesis. I’m looking at Florida because it reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, and I wanted to see what racial and ethnic patterns look like when the two classifications are not collapsed. That project lets me study one part of a larger process I care about: how institutions recognize, classify, and respond to disability-related needs.

My broader research centers the educational experiences and life-course outcomes of Black disabled children and youth from low-resource communities. I want to understand how disability identification and institutional perceptions shape children’s schooling, how K–12 experiences carry into higher education and adulthood, and which policies and institutional conditions expand young people’s agency, opportunity, and self-determination. I’m drawn to administrative and longitudinal data because they can help me follow these processes across institutions and over time.

Research agenda

Race, disability, education, and the life course

I study how racism, ableism, white supremacy, and socioeconomic inequality shape the educational experiences and life-course trajectories of Black disabled children and youth from low-resource communities. I am interested in how schools and other institutions create and respond to disability, how young people experience and navigate those institutions, and the policies and conditions that expand agency, opportunity, access, and self-determination.

Longer-term research agenda

These are the broader questions I want my research program to address over time. My current projects ask narrower empirical questions below.

01When schools decide that a child needs disability services, who gets identified, for what, and at what point in childhood?

02What happens to Black disabled students after identification: where are they placed, what support do they receive, and what expectations follow them?

03How do those school experiences shape the transition out of K–12 education and into college, work, and adult life?

04Which school and policy conditions give Black disabled young people more control over their education, support, and futures?

How I approach disabilityMy work is grounded in a social understanding of disability. I use administrative categories to study what institutions record and do without treating those categories as transparent measures of disabled people themselves.

Current projectsMy present work examines disability identification, developmental-service timing, and special-education reporting as part of a broader agenda on Black disabled young people’s educational experiences and lives.

Honors Undergraduate Thesis · University of Central Florida

When Speech and Language Diverge

Racial and Ethnic Patterns in Florida Speech and Language Impairment Program Membership, 2017–2025

01Question 05Limits

Data

Florida Department of Education administrative workbooks covering all 67 county school districts across eight school years, 2017–18 through 2024–25.

Key study decisions

Keep SI and LI separate

Florida reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, so I preserve that distinction instead of starting with the combined federal category.

Define the denominator

The primary rates use same-race PK–12 enrollment. I rerun the analysis with K–12 enrollment to see whether that choice changes the pattern.

Handle small and suppressed cells

I test alternative district-size cutoffs and bounds for suppressed enrollment cells rather than silently treating suppressed values as zero.

Methods

R · program-membership rates · risk ratios · district and year comparisons · sensitivity checks

Question

Florida reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately. Federal data combine them. I ask whether racial and ethnic patterns look different when I preserve that distinction.

Design

I built a dataset covering all 67 Florida county school districts for each school year from 2017–18 through 2024–25. I compare program-membership rates for Black and Hispanic students with White students, look at how the patterns vary across districts and years, and test whether the results change under different data choices.

Data
Florida Department of Education administrative workbooks

Scale
16 workbooks · 1,608 district-year-race rows

Methods
R · program-membership rates · risk ratios · district and year comparisons · sensitivity checks

Status
Analysis frozen and reproducible; manuscript in progress

What these data cannot tell me: program membership shows how students are recorded by the school system under particular administrative rules. I treat these categories as evidence about institutional decisions, not as transparent measures of disability itself. The data do not tell me how many students actually have a speech or language disorder, whether individual students were classified correctly, whether discrimination caused a pattern, what services a student actually received, or how students experienced those decisions.

Independent research

Who Gets Help First?

Racial and Socioeconomic Differences in the Timing of Developmental Service Entry Among U.S. Children

Study at a glance

Data
2023–2024 National Survey of Children’s Health
Sample
Children who received developmental services
Analysis
Survey weights · regression · adjusted probabilities
Missing data
Multiply imputed household-poverty measures

This project asks a question I realized I had been skipping: among children who eventually receive developmental services, who gets there earlier? I use pooled 2023–2024 National Survey of Children’s Health data to examine differences by race, ethnicity, and household poverty. The analysis accounts for the survey’s sampling design, uses the survey’s multiply imputed household-poverty measures, and compares adjusted probabilities across groups. I also run alternative versions of the analysis to see how sensitive the results are to my modeling choices.

What Federal Special-Education Data Hide

A Policy Case for Disaggregating Speech and Language Impairment

State reporting

Speech Impairment+Language Impairment

Federal reporting

Speech or Language ImpairmentOne combined category
The distinction disappears.

When two state categories are reported as one federal category, the public federal data cannot show whether patterns differ between Speech Impairment and Language Impairment.

This project grew out of a problem I ran into while working with special-education data: the federal category combines speech and language impairment even when states keep them separate. I am tracing what states actually report and what gets lost when those data are forced into one federal category. My proposed approach keeps the state’s original categories, records how each one is matched to the federal category, and says when the public documentation is not enough to make that match without guessing.
